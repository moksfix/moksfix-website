If you have ever asked a studio for a website and heard the words 'design' and 'development' used as if they mean the same thing, you are not alone. They are two different crafts that work together, and knowing where one ends and the other begins helps you brief the right people and spend your budget well. This guide explains web design vs web development in plain language, with no jargon.

## The short version

Web design is about how a site looks, feels and guides people. Web development is about building the thing so it actually works in a browser and on a server. Design decides what should happen on screen; development makes it happen.

Think of building a shop. The designer plans the layout, the lighting, the signage and the flow from the door to the till. The developer lays the wiring, plumbs the water, fits the shelves and makes sure the doors open. You need both before anyone can walk in and buy something.

## What web design covers

Web design is the visual and experience side of a project. A good designer is thinking about the person using the site at least as much as the brand showing it off.

Design work usually includes:

- **User experience (UX):** the journey a visitor takes, how pages connect, and how easy it is to get things done.
- **User interface (UI):** buttons, menus, forms and spacing, the bits people actually tap and click.
- **Visual design:** colour, typography, imagery and the overall mood.
- **Branding:** making the site look and sound like you, consistently.
- **Layout and hierarchy:** deciding what the eye sees first, second and third on every screen.

The deliverables tend to be wireframes, mockups and prototypes, often in a tool like Figma. At this stage nothing is clickable in a real sense; it is a detailed plan for how the finished site should look and behave. ## What web development covers

Web development is where the design becomes a real, working website. This is the code side, and it splits into two halves.

### Front-end (the part people see)

Front-end development turns the design into something a browser can display. It is the structure, styling and interaction you experience directly: menus that open, forms that respond, pages that fit a phone as well as a laptop. When people say front-end vs back-end, this is the front.

### Back-end (the part people don't see)

Back-end development is the engine room. It handles databases, logic, user accounts, security and connections to other systems like payment gateways or email. When you ask 'what is web development' and picture more than a static page, the back-end is usually what you are imagining: the place where data is stored, rules are enforced and real work gets done.

A brochure site may need very little back-end. A booking platform, a customer portal or an internal tool needs plenty, which is the territory of [web application development](/services/web-application-development).

## Design vs development at a glance

Here is the difference between web design and development in one view.

| | Web Design | Web Development |
|---|---|---|
| **Main focus** | How it looks, feels and guides people | How it works and holds up |
| **Core skills** | UX, UI, visual design, branding | Front-end code, back-end logic, databases |
| **Typical tools** | Figma, design systems, prototypes | HTML, CSS, JavaScript, server frameworks, databases |
| **Deliverables** | Wireframes, mockups, clickable prototypes | Working pages, features, APIs, deployed site |
| **When you need it** | Any site that must look right and be easy to use | Any site that must function, store data or scale |

Most real projects move left to right: design first, then development. The two rarely happen in neat isolation, though, which brings us to the handoff.

## How they overlap and hand off

The line between design and development is not a wall. The best results come when the two talk constantly.

- A designer needs to understand what is realistic to build, so the mockups are not beautiful but impossible.
- A developer needs to understand the intent behind a design, so the final build keeps the spirit, not just the pixels.
- Details like animations, loading states and error messages sit right on the border and need both crafts.

When design and development are handled by separate companies, this handoff is where things often go wrong. Files get lost in translation, spacing drifts, and 'small changes' turn into long email chains. A tight loop between the two saves time and money.

## Which one do you actually need?

It depends on your goal. A quick guide:

- **A brochure or marketing site** (about you, your services, a contact form): design-led, with light development. This is usually a two to four week job.
- **A web application** (logins, dashboards, bookings, payments, data): development-heavy, with design shaping the experience throughout. Apps like this ship in weekly increments, with a usable MVP typically in six to twelve weeks.
- **A redesign of something that already works:** often more design than development, unless the underlying system also needs rebuilding.

If you are not sure which camp you fall into, that is normal. Describe what you want the site to do, and a good studio will tell you honestly where the effort sits.
